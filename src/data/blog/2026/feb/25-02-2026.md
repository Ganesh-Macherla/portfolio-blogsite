Anyways

It's also very strange, because from the outside everything looked fine.

No major problems, i was doing well in my exams and grades were decent and i wasn't struggling with overwhelming anxiety like i thought i did, so yess everything was fineee.

But what went wrong you ask? 

I think I never developed that raw instinct to push myself when things got uncomfortable *(uk like every other human does in this world smh).*

Its just that I wasn't forced to. There was always a cushion, and always a fallback plan for myself. And when you grow up like that, where running away becomes an inherent instinct, self-initiative doesn't come to you naturally

You just wait for instructions. and hope someone hops along to give you motivation.

Because you think taking decision when you are comfortable is the right way to handle things so you wait for the "right time".

And that's wrong, things should not be dealt like that. And so I used to run away from all my problems, I hit the eject button on sooo many minor inconveniences that I honestly lost track. 

---

Being an introvert didn’t help either. I was always more in my head than in the moment because conversations felt exhausting and crowds felt overwhelming, and putting in so much effort just to get little or nothing back seemed pointless or at least that’s what I convinced myself into thinking. Looking back now, I know I was wrong. But at 14, with nobody really telling me the things I needed to hear, what else was I supposed to believe?

I instead chose the easier route...to stay small, quiet and thus i felt safe. That safety cost me alot, as you don’t really grow inside a comfort zone built entirely around avoidance. You just learn how to run better. Running from awkward conversations, from failure, from embarrassment, from people, and from vulnerability...until escaping becomes the only thing you know how to do.

And then one day, life puts you in a situation where running isn’t an option anymore.

That’s when you break.

Because fear hits differently when you’ve spent years avoiding it instead of facing it. Every overwhelming moment feels impossible, not because it actually is, but because you never gave yourself the chance to learn how to survive it. All those years of hiding from discomfort didn’t protect me from fear, they only made me weaker against it.

---

And honestly, the worst part wasn’t even the fear itself. It was watching everyone else move forward while I stayed stuck in the same place, mentally. People around me were learning how to talk confidently, connect with others, facing failure, and then getting back up, and becoming more comfortable with themselves. Meanwhile, I was still fighting battles entirely inside my own head. Every opportunity felt terrifying before it even began.

So naturally, I started envying people who could simply exist without overthinking every little thing. I used to think confidence was something people were just born with. That some people were naturally made for the world while others were meant to stay in the background. But over time, I realised that almost everyone feels the same way at some point. 

The difference is that most people eventually learn how to pop that bubble of self-consciousness, while I kept feeding mine. I convinced myself I was uniquely broken, uniquely awkward, and uniquely left out (as weird as it sounds). And once you start believing that, your mind begins collecting evidence for it everywhere. Every failed interaction becomes proof, silence feels personal and tiny moments of rejection stick to you harder than it should.

Meanwhile, the people I thought were “naturally confident” were probably just people who had already embarrassed themselves enough times to stop fearing it.

That’s the thing nobody tells you about building confidence. Please!! I've seen enough motivational speakers talk bullshit about their perfect little “success stories,” as if confidence suddenly appeared the moment they won something or became successful. Most of that sounds polished only because you’re hearing the final version of the story, not the messy years before it. Nobody talks enough about the awkwardness, the humiliation, and the nights spent replaying that one sentence in your head for hours. That feeling of standing in a room full of people and somehow still feeling invisible. Yup.

Real confidence usually isn’t built through winning. It’s built through surviving these moments you thought would destroy you emotionally, only to realize life keeps moving forward. It’s realizing embarrassment has an expiration date. You say something awkward and realize the world didn’t end. That rejection doesn’t kill you and you wake up just fine the next day. That people are far too busy in their own bubbles worrying about themselves to remember every mistake you make. 

And maybe that sounds obvious to some people, but when you grow up overthinking every interaction, these realizations don’t come to you naturally. You have to experience them firsthand. You have to fail, look stupid, feel uncomfortable, and still wake up the next morning realizing that shit like that can’t really break you once you’ve already lived through it.

That’s where confidence actually comes from. Doesn't it sound ironic. The very thing I avoided to protect myself, which was discomfort, was the exact thing I needed in order to grow.

Maybe I haven’t completely overcome those fears yet either. Maybe some parts of me will always hesitate before speaking, and always analyze too much, and always fear being misunderstood. But at least now I know that the only way out of that bubble is straight through it. Facing those fears head-on is probably the only thing that’ll slowly help me grow out of the person I trapped myself into becoming.

Maybe I can't magically fix myself overnight or whatever delulu main character arc nonsense in those movies that people talk about. But maybe one day I’ll notice I’m no longer scared of the things that used to control me, and maybe that alone will be enough to finally grow out of my bubble.
